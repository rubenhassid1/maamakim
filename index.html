Anne Bonny & Mary Read: Women Pirates in History, Ep. 2

MV
Michelle Vidal
Mar 11, 2026

Pirate history is full of larger-than-life figures—Blackbeard, Sir Francis Drake, Henry Morgan—men whose names have become inseparable from the Golden Age of Piracy. Their reputations loom so large that they often dominate the entire story. But if you look past the usual suspects, two women appear in the historical record who seem to have stepped straight out of a legend: Anne Bonny and Mary Read.

Both sailed under Captain "Calico Jack" Rackham in the waters around Jamaica and the Bahamas in 1720. Both fought in men's clothing. And both, remarkably, were real—their trial records still survive in the colonial archives of Jamaica.

Two Roads to the Same Deck

Anne Bonny was born in Ireland around the turn of the eighteenth century, the daughter of a lawyer who eventually relocated the family to the Carolinas. Headstrong from the start, she married a small-time sailor, drifted to Nassau—then the beating heart of Caribbean piracy—and fell in with Rackham, abandoning respectable life for the deck of a stolen sloop.

Mary Read's road was stranger still. Raised in England as a boy so her mother could collect an inheritance, she spent years living as "Mark Read"—serving as a soldier in Flanders before shipboard life carried her to the West Indies. When pirates seized the vessel she was traveling on, Read joined them. It was aboard Rackham's ship that she and Bonny discovered each other's secret.

When the moment came to fight, witnesses at their trial agreed, no one aboard fought harder than the two women.

The Last Stand off Negril Point

In October 1720, a privateer commissioned by the governor of Jamaica cornered Rackham's sloop off the western tip of the island. Most of the crew—by many accounts, deep in their cups—retreated below deck. Bonny and Read stayed topside, pistols and cutlasses drawn, reportedly cursing the men below for their cowardice.

The battle was lost anyway. The crew was hauled to Spanish Town, tried, and sentenced to hang. Rackham was executed in November 1720; legend holds that Bonny's parting words to him were withering—that had he fought like a man, he need not have died like a dog.

Pleading the Belly

Bonny and Read escaped the gallows through one of the era's few legal mercies available to women: both were found to be pregnant, and the court stayed their executions. Mary Read died in prison in April 1721, likely of fever. Anne Bonny's fate is one of piracy's enduring mysteries—the record simply goes quiet. Some historians believe her father bought her freedom and that she lived out a long, quiet life in South Carolina. No one knows for certain.

That uncertainty is part of why the two women endure. Blackbeard's story ends with his head hanging from a bowsprit. Anne Bonny's ends with a blank page—an invitation, three centuries wide, for the legend to keep growing.
